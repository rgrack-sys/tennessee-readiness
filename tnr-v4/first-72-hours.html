Preparedness Guide

The First72 Hours

When the power, phones and roads go down, the first three days set the tone for everything after. What to expect, what it means — and what you should do about it.

See the Timeline Get the Printable
The Basics

Why the First Three Days Matter

The American Red Cross recommends keeping enough food and water for two weeks at home, and three days' worth ready to go if you have to leave. That's because in a big ice storm, flood, wildfire or regional outage, police, fire, EMS and the power company can all be stretched past their limits at the same time — even when they're doing everything right.

This guide walks through what typically happens as hours turn into days, and what a household can do at each stage. The time windows are planning guides, not guarantees — every event and every place plays out differently.

Set Your Priorities

The Rule of 3s

An old survival rule of thumb for deciding what to fix first. It's a planning tool, not a stopwatch — real limits vary a great deal with age, health, weather and activity — but the order is what counts.

🌬️
3 Minutes

Without Air

Breathe. Smoke, carbon monoxide and drowning come first.

⛺
3 Hours

Without Shelter

In harsh cold, wet or heat, exposure is the next fastest threat.

💧
3 Days

Without Water

Stay hydrated. Store it, protect it, and know how to treat more.

🍴
3 Weeks

Without Food

Stay fed. Important for strength and morale, but rarely the first crisis.

Hour by Hour

What to Expect → What It Means → What to Do

Hours0–24
The Beginning of Disruption
What to Expect
What It Means
What You Should Do
ExpectConfusion, denial and conflicting information
What it meansEarly reports may be incomplete, contradictory or wrong. People may underestimate how serious it is or how long it will last.
What to doCheck information against more than one independent source. Separate confirmed facts from rumor before making big decisions.
ExpectOutside help may not arrive quickly
What it meansPolice, fire, EMS, utilities and government services can be overwhelmed even when they're still operating.
What to doShift right away toward taking care of your own household. Take stock of water, food, medication, power, fuel, shelter and first-aid supplies.
ExpectCommunications may become unreliable
What it meansCell networks can overload, internet can fail, and normal broadcast information may be limited.
What to doHave more than one way to get news. Keep a battery or hand-crank radio, a backup way to reach people, and a family communication plan.
ExpectEmergency services may be overloaded
What it meansProblems that normally get a fast response may see long delays — or none at all.
What to doKeep first-aid supplies, fire extinguishers, basic tools, critical medications and simple emergency instructions at home.
ExpectFires and accidents become harder to manage
What it meansDamage, generators, candles, camp stoves and debris create new hazards just as response capacity drops.
What to doCut unnecessary risk. Run generators outdoors only — the CDC says at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents. Control open flames, know where your extinguishers are, and know your way out.
ExpectTheft and opportunistic crime may increase
What it meansDark streets, closed businesses, distracted households and stretched law enforcement create opportunities.
What to doStay aware, secure doors and property, keep valuables out of sight, and avoid unnecessary confrontation.
ExpectFear and uncertainty grow after dark
What it meansNo lights, no news and no routine make everything feel worse at night.
What to doFinish outside tasks in daylight. Set up lighting, secure the house, organize sleeping areas and make a plan before nightfall.
ExpectUnprepared people start running short
What it meansHouseholds with little stored water, food, medication or power may quickly need help.
What to doKnow what you have, what you can reasonably share, and what has to be held back for your household's critical needs.
ExpectYou may not know how big the event is
What it meansWhether it's local, regional or national decides whether help and supplies exist nearby.
What to doWork out how widespread it is before you evacuate or spend scarce supplies. Keep reassessing as better information comes in.
ExpectA stay-or-go decision may be needed
What it meansStaying put is usually easier, but some hazards make leaving necessary.
What to doDecide your leave-now triggers ahead of time: fire, flooding, a damaged structure, a chemical or radiation hazard, extreme temperatures or another immediate threat.
Hours24–48
Shortages Become Visible
What to Expect
What It Means
What You Should Do
ExpectShortages become more visible
What it meansNormal resupply may stop, and stored supplies start to drop.
What to doMove to controlled use. Measure water, food, fuel, battery power and medical supplies instead of using them casually.
ExpectFood, water, medicine and fuel become what matters
What it meansNecessities start to outweigh comfort items as the disruption continues.
What to doPut critical resources first and protect anything that would be hard or impossible to replace.
ExpectPolice, fire, EMS and hospitals are more strained
What it meansDemand climbs while staff, fuel, communications and supplies may shrink.
What to doAvoid unnecessary travel and risk-taking. Deal with small problems early, before they become emergencies that need outside help.
ExpectMedical emergencies become more dangerous
What it meansChronic illness, injuries, oxygen, refrigerated medicine, dialysis and mobility needs get harder to support.
What to doCheck every household medical need now: medication quantities, refrigeration, backup power, first-aid supplies and special equipment.
ExpectHospitals and care facilities may lose capability
What it meansUtility failures, shortages and short staffing can reduce the care they can give.
What to doIf someone depends on facility care, line up backup arrangements and alternate locations before conditions get worse.
ExpectFear and frustration increase
What it meansPeople start to realize this may last longer than they expected.
What to doKeep a routine, give everyone a job, talk clearly as a household, and don't make decisions out of anxiety alone.
ExpectMore people start leaving the area
What it meansTraffic, fuel demand and pressure on main routes can build fast.
What to doIf you must evacuate, go before routes jam — but only with a safe destination, a workable route and enough fuel.
ExpectFuel becomes a limiting factor
What it meansStations may have no power, deliveries may stop, and what's left can disappear quickly.
What to doSave vehicle fuel. Skip unnecessary trips and keep enough for an evacuation or a real medical need.
ExpectPeople may start asking for help
What it meansFamily, neighbors and strangers may need food, water, rides, shelter, information or medical help.
What to doDecide ahead of time how your household will handle requests, and what you can share without putting your own plan at risk.
ExpectYour original plan may not be working
What it meansReal use, weather, injuries and new information can make the first plan out of date.
What to doSit down and reassess: water, food, shelter, sanitation, medical needs, power, communications, transportation and security.
Hours48–72
Conditions Deteriorate
What to Expect
What It Means
What You Should Do
ExpectSanitation problems become serious
What it meansWith water and sewer down, toilets, garbage and human waste quickly become a health hazard.
What to doSet up a backup toilet and waste plan. Keep waste well away from living space, food preparation and stored water.
ExpectDehydration becomes more dangerous
What it meansStored water may be running low while drinking, cooking, hygiene and sanitation needs continue.
What to doTrack how much water you have and how fast it's going (the American Red Cross planning figure is one gallon per person per day). Find and treat more before you run short.
ExpectHeat or cold becomes dangerous
What it meansWithout heating or cooling, a house can become unsafe — especially for children, older adults and anyone medically fragile.
What to doMove everyone into fewer rooms, use clothing and insulation smartly, set up safe heating or cooling, and pace work to the temperature.
ExpectUntreated medical problems get worse
What it meansSmall cuts, blisters, dehydration, illness, tooth trouble and missed medication can turn serious over several days.
What to doDo a quick health check on everyone each day. Treat small problems early and protect critical medicine and equipment.
ExpectHospitals may be even less able to help
What it meansPower, staff, transport, medicine, oxygen and supplies may all be limited.
What to doAssume routine medical care may not be available, and ration critical medical supplies with that in mind.
ExpectCrime or desperation may rise in hard-hit areas
What it meansScarcity plus reduced response can lead to opportunistic or desperate behavior.
What to doKeep a low profile, limit travel, stay aware, secure your home and avoid confrontation whenever you can.
ExpectRoad travel gets harder
What it meansFuel shortages, traffic, debris, closures, damaged roads and unsafe areas can all limit movement.
What to doTravel only when the benefit clearly beats the risk. Know alternate routes and keep enough fuel to get there — or get back.
ExpectDisplaced people may look for supplies and rides
What it meansPeople who can't meet basic needs may head toward homes, businesses, relief points or working vehicles.
What to doSettle your household's boundaries and how you'll help others before you're forced to decide under pressure.
ExpectSupplies keep shrinking
What it meansEven a well-prepared household can use things up faster than expected.
What to doRecount how many days of water, food, fuel, medicine and power you have left — using what you've actually used, not guesses.
ExpectGood information is worth more than ever
What it meansRumors and constant checking waste batteries and lead to bad decisions.
What to doCheck news at set times. Stick to reliable sources and save your radios, phones and batteries.
When One System Fails, Others Follow

Mitigate the Cascade

Every hour that normal systems stay down, the consequences compound. The goal is to keep one failure from causing the next. A power outage, for example, can lead to water problems, spoiled food, medication problems, sanitation trouble and lost communications.

8 Core Priorities

1Water

Losing water service hits drinking, cooking, hygiene and sanitation fast. Store water, protect it, ration it wisely, and know how to get and treat more.

2Shelter

Heat, cold, wind and rain can become life-threatening without utilities. Keep a dry, protected living area and a seasonal plan for heating or cooling.

3Medical

Small problems grow when professional care is delayed. Keep medications, special-needs supplies and first aid, and treat minor problems early.

4Sanitation

Piled-up waste can turn an outage into a health emergency. Have a toilet, hand-washing, garbage and wastewater plan that doesn't need the utilities.

5Power

Stored power and generator fuel run out. Power the critical things first and don't waste batteries or fuel on the rest.

6Information

Bad information leads to bad decisions. Use several sources, double-check anything critical, and separate fact from rumor.

7Transportation

Fuel and road access can fall off quickly. Save fuel, know where you'd go, keep alternate routes, and avoid unneeded trips.

8Security

Disruption makes any household more exposed. Stay aware, secure your home, limit exposure and avoid confrontation.

Operating Principle

Don't wait for every system to fail before acting. Spot the next likely failure, protect what it affects, and reassess at regular intervals.

⚠ Generator Safety: Carbon Monoxide Kills Generator exhaust contains carbon monoxide, a poison gas you can't see or smell. The CDC says never run a generator indoors or in a garage or carport, and always keep it outside at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents. Put battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms near sleeping areas.

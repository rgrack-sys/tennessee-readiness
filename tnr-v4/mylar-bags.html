Storing Food inMylar Bags

How to pack dry staples like plain white rice, wheat berries and dry beans so they keep their quality for years. Start with the right food, seal it right, and check it every time you rotate.

Jump to the Steps Get Mylar Bags Here
The Basics

Why Mylar Works

Stored food is ruined by oxygen, moisture, light and pests. A Mylar bag is a pouch of plastic film laminated with a thin foil layer. Heat-sealed with an oxygen absorber inside, it limits the first three, and a lidded bin around it guards against the fourth. One thing it can't do: an oxygen absorber removes oxygen, but it does not dry food, sterilize it or remove toxins. The food has to be right before it goes in.

🫧

Removes Oxygen

Oxygen turns oils rancid and fades flavor. The absorber takes oxygen out of the sealed bag; the foil layer helps keep more from getting back in.

💧

Keeps Moisture Out

The foil layer limits moisture getting in. It won't dry food that's already damp — that's why food choice comes first.

🌑

Blocks Light

Light breaks down vitamins and color. The pouch blocks it.

📦

Portion Control

Pack portions you can finish after opening, and keep the rest sealed.

Choose Your Food First

What to Store — and What Not To

Use oxygen absorbers only with shelf-stable, low-oil food verified to be dry enough. Utah State University Extension uses about 10% moisture or less as household guidance. "Shelf-stable in its retail bag," "dry to the touch" and "dry enough for low-oxygen packing" are three different things. If the label doesn't tell you, ask the supplier for the product specification — and if you still don't know, keep the food in its original packaging and follow the label.

⚠ Moisture + No Oxygen = Botulism Risk Utah State University Extension warns that botulism poisoning may result if moist products are stored in packaging that reduces oxygen. Never use this method to make wet or perishable food shelf-stable.
FoodWhat to Do
Plain polished white rice, whole wheat berries, dry beans, lentils, split peasGood first choices — when the exact product is shelf-stable, low in oil and verified dry enough. Some white rice is sold above the moisture guidance, so check the specification.
Plain rolled oats, plain dry pastaOnly if the specific product meets the same requirements. Check blends and added ingredients separately. If unsure, keep it in its original packaging and rotate it through normal use.
White flour, powdered milkKeep in the original packaging and rotate. Moisture and storage life vary by product. For long storage, buy manufacturer-packed long-storage products.
Brown rice, nuts, granola, whole-wheat flour and other higher-oil foodsNot for this method. Their oils go rancid. Store them normally, follow the package directions and rotate them sooner.
Fresh or cooked food, cheese, jerky, moist or uncertain home-dried produceKeep out. Follow that food's own safe-storage instructions. Store-bought freeze-dried foods stay sealed in their original packaging.
Plain granulated sugar, plain saltStore separately in clean, dry, tightly closed food-grade containers — no absorber. Brown sugar stays in its normal pantry packaging.

Plan past the bag: start with food your household already eats. If you store rice, beans or whole grains, plan for clean cooking water, a way to cook, and a grain mill if your recipes need flour from stored wheat berries.

Before You Start

What You'll Need

  • 🛍️
    Food-grade Mylar bagsNew, undamaged, heat-sealable foil-laminate pouches sold for dry food storage, with sealing instructions. Thicker film resists punctures, but thickness alone doesn't tell you how well a bag blocks oxygen. Choose sizes you can finish after opening.
  • 🧪
    Oxygen absorbersIntact, single-use packets made for dry food, with the maker's capacity chart. See the sizing guide below.
  • 🔥
    A heat sealer suited to your bagsAn impulse sealer is the common choice. Use the bag and sealer makers' directions for settings.
  • 🫙
    A jar with a gasketed lidTo hold unused absorbers closed until the next batch, following their instructions.
  • 🖊️
    Labels, marker and a clean, dry scoopFor filling, and for labeling every bag.
  • 🪣
    A rigid lidded binProtects bags from punctures, light and pests. Where rodents are a problem, a securely lidded metal bin. The heat seal is the barrier; the bin is armor.
Step by Step

How to Pack & Seal

Set up before you open the absorbers

Work indoors on a clean, dry surface with dry tools. Check the food for moisture, mold, pests and damage — discard anything suspect. Test your sealer on an empty bag of the same type: you want a continuous seal with no wrinkles, channels or melted holes.

Portion and label

Label each bag with the food, the packing date and the lot number from the original package if it has one. Fill it with the verified-dry food, leaving enough clear bag at the top for a clean, flat seal.

Keep dust, crumbs and kernels off the sealing area — debris there makes weak seals.

Add absorbers and seal promptly

With the food and sealer ready, open the absorber supply. Put the rated amount of intact packets in each bag, keeping them away from the seal. Close the leftover absorbers back up as their instructions direct, and seal the filled bags promptly — absorbers start working as soon as they meet air.

Inspect the whole seal once it cools

Look for one unbroken band across the full opening — no channels, wrinkles, trapped food, holes or weak edges. If you find a problem while you're still packing and the food is clean and dry, move it to a new bag with a fresh absorber.

A zipper is handy after opening, but use the heat seal for long-term storage.

Record the batch and store it

Write down the food, amount, pack date, where it's stored, when you plan to rotate it, and which bags and absorbers you used. Put the bags in the lidded bin somewhere you can easily get to and check.

Read the seal, not the shape of the bag Some bags pull tight as the absorber works, and some don't — most of the air in a bag is nitrogen, and it stays. A loose bag doesn't prove the absorber failed, and a tight bag doesn't prove the food is safe. Inspect the seal and keep track of how the bag has been stored. A bag that becomes unusually swollen should be thrown out.
Sizing Guide

How Big an Oxygen Absorber?

Your absorber maker's capacity chart comes first. Absorbers have to cover the air above the food and between the pieces — so a partly filled bag, or loose food like pasta and beans, needs more capacity than a full bag of rice or wheat berries.

After You Seal

Store, Rotate & Check

🌡️ Cool, dry, dark, off the floorUtah State University Extension gives roughly 40–70°F as a good range for food quality. In Tennessee, an interior pantry usually beats an attic or garage. Use a basement only if it stays reliably dry. Keep bins away from sun, heat, leaks, condensation and flood-prone spots.
🔄 Rotate and inspectUse the oldest suitable food first. Check bags during normal rotation and after any leak, flood or pest problem: seams, labels, punctures, moisture and swelling. Write down the date of your next check.
🐭 Guard against crittersRodents chew right through Mylar. Keep bags in a lidded bin — metal where rodents are a concern.
🥄 After openingUse clean, dry utensils and keep the rest in a clean, dry container. An opened bag starts a new, shorter storage period — follow normal storage guidance for that food. Remove the absorbers and throw them away.
⚠ When in doubt, throw it out — don't taste it Throw out home-packed bags with any of these:
  • Damp contents or condensation inside
  • Mold or insects
  • Unexplained swelling
  • Leaks or punctures that leave you unsure how the food was stored
  • Water damage or possible contact with flood water
Normal smell or appearance can't prove stored food is safe — the CDC notes you can't see, smell or taste the toxin that causes botulism. When you find pests or moisture, clean the shelf and check the bags around it.

How Long Will It Last?

There's no single answer: storage temperature, the food itself, the packaging and how it was handled all affect quality. As an example, Utah State University Extension reports that properly sealed and stored polished white rice will store well for 25 to 30 years.

Estimate only: that figure describes the quality of one food under favorable conditions. It is not an expiration date or a safety guarantee for a home-packed bag, and it doesn't carry over to other foods. Rotate on your normal schedule and keep a record of how each batch has been stored.

Try It Once

A Practical First Batch

Example A Tennessee household starts with one bag of plain polished white rice it already cooks regularly. It confirms the rice's specification, picks a meal-sized bag, matches the absorber to that bag and fill using the maker's chart, and writes down one packing date and one rotation date. At its next pantry check, it can find the bag, read the label, and judge its condition with the same checklist.
